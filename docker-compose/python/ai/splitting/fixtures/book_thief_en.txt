The Book Thief Test

Liesel Meminger was a girl who carried words she could not yet read. Her
brother had died on the train, and the memory of the funeral would not
leave her alone.

Death is a thief, and it does not knock before it enters. It simply
arrives, takes what it wants, and leaves the living to count what is
missing.

"She should not be outside," the woman said.

"It is all right," the man answered. "Come inside before the snow."

"I had a brother," the girl whispered.

"Every child loses something," the woman replied. "You are not alone in
this."

The snow kept falling. It covered the steps, the street, and the small
yard where the boy had played all those short years ago.

"Tell me. What do you see when you dream like that?" she asked.

"I see myself turning around, and waving goodbye," the man said.

"That is a good dream," the girl told him. "Only a good dream would end
with a goodbye."

"You will learn to read," the man promised. "We will start with the
alphabet, and we will not stop."

He taught her letters on the wall of the basement, one half-word at a
time. The paint was scarce, so the words had to be.

"What if I forget everything?" she asked one evening.

"You will not forget the words," he said. "They stay where they are
loved."

The months passed. The girl read in the shelter, in the basement, and
on the street when the raids let her.

"When the war is over," the woman said, "we will plant flowers where
the rubble used to be."

"And I will write a book," the girl said. "I will write it exactly as
it happened."

The word shaker walked through the trees, and every branch held a
word instead of a leaf. She climbed the tallest one and waited.

"Words are like that," the man had told her. "The right one can hold
the whole sky up."
